\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\textbf{Setup.} We evaluate PSB on five image classification streams with a
single shared head and no stored examples. Permuted
MNIST~\cite{goodfellow2013empirical} has $10$ tasks, each a fixed pixel
permutation. Rotated MNIST has $10$ tasks with rotations from $0^\circ$ to
$180^\circ$. Split MNIST~\cite{zenke2017si} has $5$ tasks on digit pairs and is
evaluated in the domain-incremental setting with a shared binary
head~\cite{vandeven2022types}. The MNIST runs use the
$784$--$100$--$100$ ReLU network behind the published Permuted MNIST
results of EWC, SI and VCL~\cite{nguyen2018vcl}.
Split CIFAR-10 follows the class-incremental protocol of
Mazur et al.~\cite{mazur2021target}: five two-class tasks, a single
head expanding from two to ten outputs, their convolutional classifier
and 12 training epochs per task with batches of 128. We report
validation accuracy for this
stream. Split CIFAR-100 follows the class-incremental protocol of
van de Ven et al.~\cite{vandeven2022types}: ten ten-class tasks, a
shared 100-class head, a frozen five-layer convolutional feature
extractor pretrained on CIFAR-10 and two 2,000-unit ReLU layers.
Each task uses 5,000 training iterations with batches of 256; we report
test accuracy for this stream.
We run online EWC~\cite{schwarz2018progress}, SI~\cite{zenke2017si}
and VCL~\cite{nguyen2018vcl} on the MNIST streams. We also report
published results, including function-space methods that store examples
from past tasks~\cite{titsias2020frcl,pan2020fromp,rudner2022sfsvi}
and progressive networks~\cite{rusu2016progressive}, which add a
network column for every task. PSB uses $K=128$ particles. Within each
stream, the methods we run use the same batch size and number of gradient
evaluations per task, and their hyperparameters are selected by grid
searches of equal size. With $a_{i,j}$ the accuracy on task $j$ after
learning task $i$, we report the final average accuracy
$A_T=\frac1T\sum_{j}a_{T,j}$ and the average forgetting
$F_T=\frac{1}{T-1}\sum_{j<T}\big(\max_{j\le i<T}a_{i,j}-a_{T,j}\big)$. Our results
are means and standard deviations over $20$ paired seeds, where paired runs
share the task order and the initial networks. All runs execute on Cloud TPU
v6e, where the $K$ networks of a run are trained in parallel, and the evaluation
trains $47{,}361$ networks in total.

\textbf{Comparison with CL methods.} Table~\ref{tab:main_results} shows that
PSB's clearest strength is Permuted MNIST: it leads the listed methods that
use neither stored examples nor task identifiers, and is competitive with
several methods that use a coreset or task-specific components. This result
does not extend uniformly across MNIST streams. On Rotated MNIST, PSB is
comparable to online EWC, while on domain-incremental Split MNIST it remains
behind VCL. The larger particle populations therefore do not guarantee
an advantage over other replay-free methods across changes in task
structure.

On class-incremental Split CIFAR-10 and Split CIFAR-100, PSB outperforms
the EWC and SI baselines under the corresponding evaluation protocols,
but its final accuracy remains limited. Thus, retaining a population of
weight hypotheses improves on parameter-importance regularization without
fully addressing discrimination among classes learned at different
times~\cite{vandeven2022types}.
The higher published CIFAR results for FROMP and S-FSVI use coresets
and different architectures or training protocols, so they indicate what other
configurations can achieve rather than establishing a controlled ranking
against PSB. Published Split MNIST results also use class-incremental
single-head or multi-head evaluation, neither of which matches our
domain-incremental binary-head setting~\cite{swaroop2019improving,rudner2022sfsvi}.

\textbf{Ablations.} Table~\ref{tab:ablations} removes one component of PSB at a
time. With a single particle, the carried posterior reduces to the Gaussian of
the online EWC objective (\ref{eq:ewc}), and accuracy drops by $3.9$
points. Fixing $\beta=1$ applies the full likelihood from the first step and
loses $2.1$ points at four times the step budget of PSB. With $K=32$,
applying the full likelihood of a new task in one step leaves an effective
number of $1.4$ particles, which shows why the tempered posteriors are
needed. Replacing
the Fisher-metric reference by an isotropic one raises forgetting from
$2.1$ to $2.9$ points, since the bridge then moves weights that
earlier tasks constrain as freely as unconstrained ones. Reference-only
transport, which runs the annealed Langevin transport of
Section~\ref{sec:langevin} at every boundary, reaches $93.6$, below PSB
with four times as many Langevin steps. A single IMF round, which reduces to
bridge matching, recovers most of the accuracy, and the remaining rounds add
$0.4$ points.

\textbf{Bridge quality and cost.} Over $3$ IMF rounds, the energy distance
between simulated endpoints and the terminal population falls from
$0.41$ to $0.07$. When the fitted drift guides the next boundary, the
effective number of particles stays above $0.8K$, compared with
$0.3K$ for reference-only transport, and weight resets fall from
$11.2$ to $1.6$ per boundary. The fitted drift therefore transfers
across boundaries and does most of the transport, while the Langevin steps only
correct the remainder. PSB stores $K$ networks, one diagonal precision and the
two drift networks, a state that does not grow with the number of tasks, and
the cost of a boundary falls by a factor of $2.7$ relative to
reference-only transport, including the cost of fitting the bridge.
